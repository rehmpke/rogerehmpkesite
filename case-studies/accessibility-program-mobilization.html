The problem

Kankakee Community College had long considered accessibility within Web Services, especially for public-facing websites and digital content. Website templates, content practices, and digital publishing decisions were generally approached with accessibility in mind when practical opportunities existed.

As awareness of evolving DOJ Title II accessibility requirements increased, it became clear that accessibility could not be managed as a web-only issue. The institution's digital ecosystem included public websites, CMS-managed content, PDFs, digital forms, videos, email communications, Microsoft 365 content, third-party systems, and department-managed resources.

Accessibility responsibilities were distributed across academic, administrative, and student service areas. Content owners, system owners, vendors, and operational departments all influenced the accessibility of the institution's digital environment, but no centralized framework existed to coordinate ownership, intake, prioritization, documentation, vendor review, or reporting.

What initially appeared to be a web accessibility concern quickly revealed itself as an institutional governance challenge.

My role

At the time the initiative began, I served as Senior Coordinator of Web Design and Development. My formal role centered on web design, development, content management, digital communications, and stewardship of the college's public web presence.

The accessibility initiative required work beyond traditional web management. As the scope became clearer, I began researching the potential impact of DOJ Title II requirements and evaluating how accessibility responsibilities extended across websites, documents, forms, communications, media, vendor systems, and departmental content practices.

I helped shape the accessibility program framework, developed governance and reporting structures, supported remediation planning, coordinated implementation efforts across institutional functions, and worked with stakeholders to establish a sustainable accessibility operating model.

My focus was not to position Web Services as the owner of all accessibility work. The more durable goal was to create the structure, visibility, and workflows needed for accessibility to become a shared institutional responsibility.

Early discovery and awareness

The initiative did not begin as a formal compliance project. Early awareness emerged through vendor outreach and industry discussions related to digital accessibility, including conversations around accessible campus mapping technologies and other higher education platforms.

Those external signals prompted deeper research into the DOJ Title II accessibility requirements and their implications for public entities and higher education institutions. As the requirements were reviewed, the issue expanded from a single product or website concern into a much larger operational question.

The institution needed to understand not only whether individual web pages were accessible, but how accessibility would be governed across documents, forms, videos, vendor platforms, communications, training, and future procurement activity.

The critical shift was recognizing that accessibility compliance would require an operating model, not just a remediation checklist.

The accessibility landscape

Accessibility responsibilities touched several layers of the college's digital environment. Each layer carried different ownership, technical constraints, and remediation considerations.

Public web and CMS

Accessibility work included KCC.edu, program pages, public-facing templates, CloudCannon content workflows, Jekyll-generated pages, and related web publishing practices.

Documents and forms

PDFs, downloadable documents, Jotform-based digital forms, and department-published resources required inventory, review, prioritization, and remediation planning.

Media and systems

YouTube content, captioning needs, HTML email communications, Microsoft 365 resources, and third-party vendor systems became part of the broader accessibility scope.

Decision point

Several approaches were possible, but each carried different governance, risk, and sustainability tradeoffs.

Option 1: Treat it as web remediation

This would have focused on correcting website issues, but it would not have addressed PDFs, forms, media, vendor systems, training, or distributed content ownership.

Option 2: Attempt broad remediation immediately

This would have created visible activity quickly, but without governance and prioritization it risked becoming fragmented, reactive, and difficult to sustain.

Option 3: Build the operating model first

The final approach prioritized governance, intake, tracking, vendor documentation, prioritization, training, and distributed accountability before scaling remediation.

The accessibility operating model

The core of the initiative was the creation of an accessibility operating model. This became the equivalent of an institutional framework for managing accessibility work across systems, content types, departments, and vendors.

The model separated accessibility into several connected layers: governance, intake, tracking, remediation, vendor review, training, and continuous reporting. This made the work easier to explain, assign, monitor, and sustain.

Governance layer

Accessibility Coordination Group, executive visibility, ownership expectations, escalation paths, institutional communication, and shared accountability.

Operational layer

Accessibility intake, issue triage, dashboard tracking, remediation status, training visibility, and recurring progress reporting.

Execution layer

Website remediation, document review, form accessibility, media planning, vendor review, procurement support, and department-level adoption.

Governance and coordination

A cross-functional accessibility coordination structure was established to support planning, communication, and execution. The structure included representation from Web Services, Information Technology Services, Institutional Research and reporting functions, Library services, instructional support functions, department-level content owners, and other operational stakeholders.

The purpose was not simply to hold meetings. The purpose was to create a recurring structure for identifying accessibility concerns, clarifying ownership, aligning remediation work, tracking progress, escalating unresolved questions, and keeping leadership aware of institutional progress.

Governance responsibilities

  • Coordinate accessibility planning
  • Clarify ownership expectations
  • Support escalation and prioritization
  • Maintain visibility across workstreams
  • Connect accessibility to institutional risk management

Participating functions

  • Web Services and digital strategy
  • Information Technology Services
  • Institutional reporting functions
  • Library and archival content support
  • Student service, academic, and administrative areas

Accessibility intake and workflow management

As the program developed, accessibility work needed a consistent way to enter the system. Informal email requests, one-off conversations, and scattered remediation notes were not enough to support an institution-wide program.

A centralized intake and workflow process was developed to support accessibility requests, issue reporting, triage, assignment, escalation, and follow-up. This gave the institution a clearer path for handling accessibility concerns and helped separate urgent barriers from lower-risk cleanup work.

Intake

Accessibility concerns and requests could be routed through a defined process rather than scattered across informal communication channels.

Triage

Issues could be reviewed based on user impact, content type, audience, visibility, risk, and ownership before remediation work began.

Follow-up

Requests could be tracked, escalated, documented, and connected to broader remediation or training needs.

Accessibility dashboard and program tracking

Visibility became one of the most important requirements of the program. Accessibility work was happening across multiple areas, but without consolidated tracking it would have been difficult to understand progress or communicate status.

A centralized accessibility dashboard and supporting trackers were developed to consolidate program activity. The dashboard helped connect web remediation efforts, PDF review activity, accessibility requests, training participation, vendor documentation, media accessibility planning, and other program workstreams into a more coherent reporting structure.

This allowed accessibility to be managed as an operational program rather than a series of isolated tasks.

Program visibility

Dashboard views helped show active workstreams, remediation categories, training activity, and overall program movement.

Prioritization

Tracking systems supported risk-based decision-making across websites, documents, forms, media, and vendor platforms.

Reporting

Program data could be organized for internal updates, leadership awareness, planning discussions, and ongoing governance review.

Defining defensible compliance

One of the most important strategic decisions was defining how to approach compliance in a practical, risk-aligned way. The goal was not to suggest that every historical issue could be remediated at once. The goal was to establish a defensible, measurable, and sustainable posture.

Prioritization focused on student-facing services, high-traffic public content, required institutional information, high-impact accessibility barriers, and systems with broad institutional reach. This helped direct limited time and resources toward content and systems most likely to affect users.

Accessibility at institutional scale is not achieved through full remediation under deadline pressure. It is achieved through governance, prioritization, system design, and continuous execution.

Web, forms, and content remediation

Accessibility review and remediation expanded across public-facing web properties and web publishing workflows. Work included public website pages, CMS-managed templates, program content, form experiences, and recurring digital communications.

Tools and methods included Lighthouse, Pa11y, Screaming Frog, manual review, CMS template updates, content cleanup, and accessibility checks within web production workflows. This helped identify template-level issues, content-level barriers, and recurring publishing patterns that needed more sustainable correction.

Digital forms were also included in scope, especially Jotform-based workflows used across departments. This helped connect accessibility expectations to forms that supported student service, administrative, and public-facing processes.

Document and PDF remediation strategy

Documents represented one of the most complex categories of work. Like many institutions, the college had a long history of PDF-based publishing across departments, programs, forms, reports, policies, and public-facing resources.

The strategy was not simply to remediate every PDF in isolation. The program began distinguishing between documents that needed to remain PDFs, documents that could be converted to web-first content, and documents that could be retired, consolidated, or replaced by more accessible formats.

Internal remediation workflows were developed alongside vendor-supported remediation planning. CommonLook and external remediation partners were evaluated and used where appropriate for higher priority documents. This created a more scalable path than relying on one-off manual fixes alone.

Internal pathways

  • Document inventory and review
  • Priority identification
  • CommonLook-supported remediation
  • HTML alternatives where appropriate
  • Department guidance and workflow support

Vendor-supported pathways

  • Remediation partner sampling
  • High-priority document planning
  • Cost and capacity evaluation
  • Batch remediation strategy
  • Quality review and documentation

Media, captions, and communications

Accessibility work also expanded into video, media, and communications. Institutional YouTube content, captioning expectations, recorded media, and public-facing communications were included in the broader accessibility scope.

The program helped frame media accessibility as an ongoing category of work rather than a completed one-time task. Planning included captioning, remediation strategy, and coordination around media practices that affect students and the public.

HTML email communications and newsletter-style content were also reviewed through an accessibility lens, reinforcing that accessibility applied to recurring communications as well as static web pages.

Vendor accessibility governance

Accessibility responsibilities extended beyond internally managed content. Many important services depended on third-party systems, which meant vendor accessibility documentation needed to become part of the program.

A centralized SharePoint-based repository was developed to organize VPATs, Accessibility Conformance Reports, vendor contacts, review status, notes, and related documentation. This gave the institution a more consistent way to collect, review, and maintain vendor accessibility evidence.

Accessibility requirements were also incorporated into vendor evaluation and procurement workflows, including requests for documentation during new system reviews and renewals where appropriate.

Documentation

VPATs, ACRs, accessibility statements, vendor contacts, and supporting materials were organized in a centralized repository.

Review

Vendor materials could be reviewed for accessibility posture, documentation quality, known limitations, and areas requiring follow-up.

Procurement support

Accessibility documentation became part of vendor conversations for new systems, renewals, and institutional technology decisions.

Training and adoption

Long-term accessibility required more than centralized remediation. It required greater awareness and practical capability among the people creating, maintaining, and approving content.

Training was deployed across faculty and staff populations, with completion tracked through institutional learning systems. Training efforts helped establish a baseline of accessibility awareness while reinforcing that content creators and departments share responsibility for the accessibility of their materials.

Training adoption was supported by practical guidance, department-level conversations, documentation, and ongoing communication about how accessibility applied to everyday work.

Staff training

185 of 223 staff completed accessibility training, creating measurable visibility into staff participation and baseline adoption.

Faculty training

58 of 178 faculty completed accessibility training, with completion tracked through the institution's learning systems.

Cross-functional participation

The accessibility program required participation across academic, administrative, technical, and student service areas. No single department controlled every system, content type, or workflow affected by accessibility requirements.

Coordination involved Web Services, Information Technology Services, Institutional Research and reporting functions, Library services, student service departments, academic program areas, administrative offices, content owners, procurement-related stakeholders, and vendor contacts.

This cross-functional participation helped move accessibility from a technical specialty into a broader institutional responsibility.

Results

Governance

Established an institutional accessibility governance framework with coordination, ownership expectations, escalation pathways, and executive visibility.

Operations

Implemented intake, triage, dashboard tracking, remediation workflows, training visibility, and reporting infrastructure.

Vendor review

Created a centralized VPAT and accessibility documentation repository and incorporated accessibility review into vendor evaluation conversations.

Before and after

The most meaningful change was not a single remediation count or tool implementation. It was the shift from isolated accessibility activity to a coordinated institutional operating model.

Before

  • Accessibility work was largely decentralized
  • Ownership expectations varied by department
  • No formal intake process existed
  • Vendor documentation was not centrally managed
  • Progress reporting was limited
  • Remediation efforts were often reactive

After

  • Governance structures were established
  • Accessibility workflows became operational
  • Vendor review processes were defined
  • Dashboard systems provided visibility
  • Accessibility responsibilities became more clearly distributed
  • Accessibility became part of institutional planning and decision-making

Long-term impact

The accessibility initiative became far more than a compliance effort.

Rather than focusing solely on correcting individual accessibility issues, the program established governance structures, accountability models, reporting systems, vendor oversight practices, training initiatives, and operational workflows that continue to support accessibility across the institution.

Accessibility evolved from a collection of isolated remediation activities into an ongoing institutional capability embedded within daily operations. The initiative also helped establish accessibility as a strategic consideration within content management, digital communications, vendor evaluation, procurement discussions, training, and long-term planning.

The durable outcome was not simply a set of corrected pages or documents. It was a framework for helping the institution manage accessibility as an ongoing operational responsibility.

Looking ahead

The strongest next step is continued stewardship: maintaining governance practices, refining dashboard visibility, supporting departments, expanding training adoption, continuing high-priority remediation, strengthening vendor review, and reinforcing accessibility as part of routine digital operations.

The foundation established through this initiative positions accessibility as an ongoing institutional responsibility rather than a deadline-driven project.
