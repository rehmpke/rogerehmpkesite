Context

Accessibility had long been a consideration within Web Services, particularly for public-facing websites and digital content. Efforts generally focused on improving accessibility where practical opportunities existed, but no formal institution-wide accessibility program had been established.

Early awareness of the evolving DOJ Title II accessibility requirements emerged through vendor discussions and industry communications related to digital accessibility and higher education technology. While evaluating accessibility-related projects and platform considerations, it became clear that accessibility obligations extended far beyond websites alone.

Further review revealed that accessibility responsibilities touched nearly every area of the institution's digital ecosystem, including websites, forms, documents, media, communications, and third-party systems.

At the same time, existing operational challenges became more apparent. Content ownership was distributed across departments, accessibility practices varied, and no centralized framework existed for governance, prioritization, reporting, or accountability.

What initially appeared to be a web accessibility concern quickly revealed itself as a broader institutional challenge requiring a coordinated and sustainable response.

The problem

The institution faced a challenge common to many colleges and universities: accessibility responsibilities were distributed across multiple departments, content owners, systems, and technologies.

Public websites, digital forms, PDFs, videos, email communications, and third-party platforms each presented different accessibility considerations. While accessibility improvements were occurring in various areas, no centralized operational framework existed to coordinate those efforts.

  • No centralized accessibility governance structure
  • No formal intake or escalation process
  • Large inventory of legacy digital content requiring evaluation
  • Distributed ownership across academic and administrative departments
  • Inconsistent accessibility practices across content creators
  • Limited visibility into remediation progress and institutional risk
  • No centralized process for reviewing and maintaining vendor accessibility documentation

Without a coordinated program, accessibility efforts risked becoming reactive, fragmented, and difficult to sustain.

My role

At the time of the initiative, I served as Senior Coordinator of Web Design and Development.

Although accessibility-related work had historically been associated with Web Services, the scope of the emerging requirements extended far beyond traditional web management responsibilities.

As awareness of the DOJ requirements increased, I began researching their potential impact on the institution and evaluating how accessibility obligations would affect websites, documents, forms, communications, media, and third-party systems.

As the initiative expanded, I helped shape the program framework, coordinated implementation efforts across departments, developed governance and reporting structures, supported remediation planning, and worked with institutional stakeholders to establish a sustainable accessibility model.

While remediation work occurred across many areas of the institution, my primary focus centered on building the systems, processes, and governance structures necessary to support accessibility at scale.

Early discovery and awareness

The initiative did not begin as a formal compliance project.

Initial awareness emerged through vendor outreach and industry discussions related to digital accessibility. Conversations surrounding accessibility-focused solutions, including campus mapping technologies and other higher education platforms, highlighted evolving accessibility expectations and increased attention on DOJ requirements.

As research continued, it became clear that the implications extended well beyond a single website or application.

The more the institution's digital ecosystem was evaluated, the more interconnected the challenge became. Public websites, PDFs, online forms, video content, email communications, vendor systems, and department-managed resources all represented potential areas requiring accessibility consideration.

What began as a technology discussion evolved into an institutional governance challenge.

The question was no longer how to make a specific system accessible. The question became how to create an operational model capable of supporting accessibility across an entire institution.

Decision point

As accessibility requirements became clearer, several possible approaches emerged.

Option 1: Remediate everything immediately

One approach focused on correcting as many accessibility issues as possible within a short period of time. While appealing in theory, the volume of content, distributed ownership, and diversity of systems made broad remediation difficult to sustain without additional structure.

Option 2: Treat accessibility as a web-only issue

This would have limited the work to public website remediation, but the emerging scope included documents, forms, media, vendor systems, communications, and department-managed resources.

Option 3: Build governance first

The final approach focused on governance, accountability, workflows, reporting, and prioritization before scaling remediation across the institution.

The program ultimately prioritized governance first. This decision shaped every aspect of the initiative moving forward.

Building the accessibility operating model

The operating model was designed to make accessibility manageable across a decentralized institution. The goal was not to centralize every remediation task inside Web Services. The goal was to create a structure that could coordinate work, clarify ownership, support reporting, and help departments participate in long-term accessibility practices.

Governance

Created a cross-functional coordination model with shared accountability, executive visibility, escalation paths, and ongoing program oversight.

Operations

Developed intake, tracking, triage, remediation monitoring, and reporting practices to move accessibility work from informal requests into managed workflows.

Adoption

Supported training, guidance, documentation, and departmental participation so accessibility could become a shared institutional responsibility.

Governance and coordination

One of the earliest priorities involved establishing a governance structure capable of supporting accessibility across multiple departments and systems.

A cross-functional accessibility coordination model was developed to bring together stakeholders responsible for content creation, technology systems, reporting, operational support, and institutional communications.

Governance framework

  • Defined ownership expectations

Institutional participation

Accessibility became an institutional responsibility rather than the responsibility of a single department.

Accessibility intake and workflow management

As accessibility efforts expanded, informal requests and ad hoc communication became increasingly difficult to manage.

To address this challenge, structured intake and workflow processes were developed to support accessibility reporting, issue management, prioritization, and follow-up activities.

Intake

Centralized accessibility concerns and requests so issues could be routed, reviewed, and tracked more consistently.

Triage

Created a pathway for evaluating issues, identifying ownership, and escalating items when additional institutional support was needed.

Follow-up

Supported remediation monitoring, stakeholder communication, status updates, and progress reporting.

These systems transformed accessibility from an informal activity into a managed operational function.

Accessibility dashboard and program tracking

As the program matured, visibility became increasingly important.

To support planning and decision-making, a centralized accessibility dashboard and supporting tracking systems were developed to monitor remediation activity, document review efforts, training participation, vendor documentation, and overall program progress.

Visibility

Created a clearer view of program status, remediation activity, and outstanding operational categories.

Prioritization

Supported risk-based decisions about which issues, systems, and content types required earlier attention.

Reporting

Provided more consistent updates for program coordination, leadership awareness, and long-term monitoring.

Rather than relying on isolated spreadsheets and departmental updates, accessibility efforts could be viewed as part of a coordinated institutional program.

Defining defensible compliance

One of the most important strategic decisions involved defining what successful accessibility execution would look like.

Rather than pursuing complete remediation of every accessibility issue simultaneously, the program adopted a risk-based prioritization model.

Priority areas

  • Student-facing content
  • High-traffic resources
  • Required institutional information
  • High-impact accessibility barriers
  • Systems with broad institutional reach

Operating posture

  • Risk-based prioritization
  • Measurable progress
  • Sustainable remediation
  • Documented workflows
  • Continuous improvement
The objective was not perfection. The objective was establishing a defensible, measurable, and sustainable compliance posture.

Auditing and remediation operations

Accessibility review and remediation efforts expanded across multiple content types and platforms.

Web and CMS

Accessibility work included public-facing website content, CMS templates, program pages, navigation patterns, and other high-visibility web experiences.

Documents and forms

PDFs, downloadable documents, and digital forms became priority categories for review, tracking, and remediation planning.

Media and communications

Video, captioning, email communications, HTML newsletters, and related publishing practices became part of the broader accessibility scope.

Accessibility work ultimately spanned all major public-facing digital systems and student-facing tools across the institution.

The initiative also encouraged movement toward more sustainable accessibility practices, including greater use of web-based content where appropriate and reduction of unnecessary document dependencies.

Vendor accessibility governance

Accessibility responsibilities extended beyond institutionally managed content.

Many critical services relied on third-party vendors and software providers, making vendor accessibility an important component of the overall program.

To support this work, processes were established for collecting, reviewing, and maintaining vendor accessibility documentation.

Documentation

Established centralized tracking for VPATs, Accessibility Conformance Reports, and related vendor accessibility evidence.

Review

Created a process for identifying accessibility risks, documenting concerns, and supporting mitigation planning.

Procurement awareness

Integrated accessibility considerations into broader vendor evaluation and procurement discussions.

A centralized repository was established to maintain accessibility documentation and support ongoing review activities.

Long-term impact

The accessibility initiative became far more than a compliance effort.

Rather than focusing solely on correcting individual accessibility issues, the program established governance structures, accountability models, reporting systems, vendor oversight practices, training initiatives, and operational workflows that continue to support accessibility across the institution.

Accessibility evolved from a collection of isolated remediation activities into an ongoing institutional capability embedded within daily operations.

The initiative also helped establish accessibility as a strategic consideration within content management, digital communications, vendor evaluation, procurement discussions, and long-term planning efforts.

Several years from now, the most important outcome will not be the number of pages remediated or documents reviewed. The most important outcome will be the existence of a sustainable framework capable of supporting accessibility long after the original compliance deadlines have passed.

Looking ahead

Future efforts continue to focus on legacy content remediation, accessibility maturity across departments, vendor accessibility accountability, expanded training and adoption, continuous monitoring and reporting, and long-term compliance sustainability.

The foundation established through this initiative positions accessibility as an ongoing institutional responsibility rather than a deadline-driven project.
